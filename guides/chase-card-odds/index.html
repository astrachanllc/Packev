What are the real odds of pulling the Cooper Flagg card?

Every ripping platform leads with its grails. Arena Club puts a Cooper Flagg rookie at the top of a $100 basketball pack; Clutch Packs dangles a $130,000 Tom Brady. Here is what the published numbers actually say about your chances of seeing either.

The Cooper Flagg PSA 9 in Arena Club's Grail tier is listed at 0.02%, or 1 in 5,000 packs. At $100 a pack that is $500,000 of packs for one expected hit, and $346,539 for a coin-flip chance. Meanwhile a Cooper Flagg PSA 10 sits twice in the same pack's common tier at 1.96% each, so "pulling a Cooper Flagg" is about 1 in 25 — roughly 197 times more likely than pulling the one on the poster.

The grail maths

Arena Club publishes a hit rate against every individual card, which makes this one of the few chase cards in the category whose odds can be checked at all. On the $100 Basketball Gold pack the three Grail cards are listed at 0.02% each: a Victor Wembanyama PSA 10, a Jalen Brunson BGS 9.5, and the Cooper Flagg PSA 9.

There is no point at which it becomes due. Each pack is independent of the last, so 4,999 misses do not improve the odds on the 5,000th. The 1-in-5,000 figure describes a long-run frequency, not a queue you are advancing through. If you open ten packs you have a 0.2% chance, and opening ten more leaves you with a 0.4% chance in total rather than a better one.

The whole Grail tier is barely more likely. Three cards at 0.02% each is 0.06% combined, or 1 in 1,667 packs. The Chase tier, eight cards at 0.25%, comes to 2%, which is roughly one pack in fifty. Everything else — 98% of pulls — comes from the Lineup.

The same player in two different tiers

Here is the part worth understanding before you chase anything. On that Basketball Gold checklist, Cooper Flagg appears three times:

A lower-graded copy sits in the rarest tier while two higher-graded copies sit in the commonest. That is not a contradiction — almost certainly these are entirely different cards, a scarce parallel or autograph in the Grail slot and base rookies in the Lineup. But you cannot tell that from the checklist, because the checklist lists only a player name and a grade. No set, no year, no card number.

So a buyer looking at that page sees Cooper Flagg three times and has no way to work out that one of those entries is worth perhaps a hundred times the other two. The same problem blocked us from pricing Arena Club's packs at all: player plus grade is not enough to identify a card, which is why we cannot publish an expected value for any Arena Club pack. For a chase card, it means the name on the marketing is not the thing you are likely to pull.

Only one of these has been observed to land. Clutch Packs records every opening on-chain, and the $130,000 Tom Brady Refractor shows up as the maximum recorded pull on its Multisport Ascent pack — once, across 21,127 pulls. That is the only top prize in the category with any evidence behind it, and at that observed rate it implies roughly $2.1 million of packs per hit. A $35,000 LeBron Exquisite sits in the same series.

Four of the seven publish no odds on their top prize at all. Tilt Rips lists a $90,000 card on a $100 pack with no probability attached anywhere. Crown Rips gives a $100,000 maximum with no rates. A ceiling without a rate tells you what is theoretically in the building, not what you are buying.

The chase barely moves the expected value

This is the counterintuitive part, and it is the single most useful thing to know. A six-figure top prize is nearly irrelevant to what a pack returns on average.

On Courtyard's $5 Pokémon pack, the top two tiers combined contribute about 19 cents to the pack's average value. Packz's 0.1% top tier contributes a similarly small slice. The money in these products sits in the common tiers, which is exactly how a pack can advertise a 1,300x ceiling and still return 95 cents on the dollar. If you are buying for value, the grail is a rounding error.

The two exceptions prove it. Clutch Packs' Multisport Ascent posts a 1.07 times average return, but that single $130,000 Brady adds $6.15 per pack across 21,127 recorded pulls, which is 6.2% of the pack price. Strip it out and Ascent returns about 1.00 times. Its Moonshot pack is in the same position. When a jackpot does move the average, it moves it because it actually landed, not because it was advertised.

So what should you actually do

If you want the chase, buy it knowingly. High-variance packs exist and are honestly labelled on some platforms: Courtyard's Spicy packs drop their floor to around half the pack price in exchange for a much higher ceiling, at the same average return. That is a real, disclosed trade between floor and ceiling. Paying for variance is a legitimate choice; just know that is what you are buying.

If you want value, ignore the grails entirely and look at the floor, the buyback and the share of pulls below 1x. On the only platform publishing observed results, 74% of recorded pulls came back worth less than the pack price. That number tells you far more about your next pack than any ceiling does.

And check whether the card you are chasing is even identifiable. If a checklist gives you a player and a grade but no set, year or certificate number, you cannot look up what it is worth, and you cannot tell the grail apart from its common namesake in the same pack.

A note on chasing

A 1-in-5,000 card is not a goal, it is a lottery ticket attached to a product that loses money on average. Decide what you are willing to spend before you start, treat it as the cost of the entertainment, and do not buy another pack to recover the last one — the odds on the next pack are identical regardless of what just happened.
